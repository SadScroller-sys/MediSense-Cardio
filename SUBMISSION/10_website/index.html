When the ECG photo is bad, which numbers can you still trust?

We damaged ECG pages in controlled ways, photographed them with a phone, and compared what an open-source digitizer reads back with the true recording. Then we checked each measurement on its own and kept only the ones that passed rules we wrote before the test. Negative results are reported as measured. Updated 18 November 2026.

How it was tested

One fixed digitizer, a known right answer, and rules written first

Real recordings from PTB-XL are drawn as paper-style pages, damaged or photographed, and read back by Open-ECG-Digitizer, used unchanged. The same code measures the true and the digitized signal, and the two are compared.

Reference values for intervals and axis come from a commercial algorithm (12SL). Where the two commercial algorithms disagree with each other, we did not attempt the measurement.

Calibration could only shift a value, never rescale it, because rescaling hides errors by pulling values toward the average.

Warning the user

A traffic light computed from the digitized signal

The light was frozen before any real photo was scored. Below: real competition photos that gave a reading, and how often the heart rate was wrong under each light.

GREEN means no warning was raised. It is not a guarantee. RED means the read looks unreliable and says nothing about the heart. The check also flags irregular rhythms more often, because a truly irregular rhythm looks like a failed read.

A fix that held up

Flatten the page before reading it

The fix finds the page in the photo and maps it to a standard rectangle. We tested it once on 40 new phone photos of 20 new pages, with the pass rule fixed before the photos existed. Each dot is one photo; red dots gave no usable heart rate or a wrong one.

Photo as taken
14 / 40
After straightening
2 / 40

12 photos became good and none became bad (exact paired test, p = 0.0005). One phone, one laptop screen, 20 recordings. The two remaining bad readings come from one page that the digitizer misreads whatever the photo.

A larger test used 120 simulated phone photos (made by a script, not a camera). Straightening cut bad readings from 64 to 21. One of 71 GREEN readings on the straightened photos was still wrong, so GREEN is not a guarantee.

Patterns and fresh tests

What the networks trained on public data could and could not do

Small networks were trained only on public recordings (PTB-XL and CODE-15%), never on our own photos. We tested them on fresh sets of dataset pictures: drawn pages and simulated phone photos made from recordings the networks had never seen. The rule for "shown": at least 20 positives, AUROC at least 0.80 on drawn pages and at least 0.75 on simulated photos, and sensitivity at least 0.60 with specificity at least 0.90 at a threshold frozen beforehand.

The risk question

A risk percentage: tried three times, shown only as a labelled research range

A patient-facing page invites the question "how large is my risk?". We trained the same kind of network on CODE-15% (patients with a 10-second ECG and a known outcome) and wrote the pass rules before each run.

AttemptResultWhy
1. One year, ECG onlyFailedAUROC 0.71 against 0.80 for age alone; calibration slope 0.62.
2. Five years, ECG plus age, new sealed testFailedAUROC 0.83, but age alone 0.82: the ECG added +0.013, not significant.
3. Five-fold cross-validation, 19,048 patientsFailed one ruleAUROC 0.81 against 0.80 for age alone; the ECG now adds +0.013 (0.006 to 0.021). Calibration slope 0.76; the rule asks 0.8. Not a blind test.

So no validated risk is shown. The demo has a separate box, clearly labelled research only, that shows a wide range for a GREEN straightened photo. The age is typed, taken from the dataset record, or estimated from the ECG (average error 9.3 years; the rules for using that were written before the run). It averages the five cross-validation models.

How far to trust the range: it contained the value of the clean ECG for 88 to 97% of drawn pages but only 61 to 73% of photographs (target 80%, not met), so with a photo the real uncertainty is larger than shown. An internal check on the same patients (cross-validated, not external) found the displayed probability well calibrated (within 5 points in all 10 deciles of predicted risk, although the top decile was overestimated) and ECG plus age ahead of age plus sex in both sexes and every age band, by a small margin (AUROC 0.811 against 0.804). External validation on a fresh cohort is future work. The numbers also run higher than for people in general, because only people who died or were followed for five years are in the data. It is not a diagnosis and not medical advice.

What this does not show

Limits we state up front

  • One digitizer, two phones (ours and a friend's), mostly photos of a screen. Printed paper from one other phone was tested on few photos.
  • Damage is simulated on computer-drawn pages, and the digitizer was trained on a similar generator.
  • Real captures contain fewer than 100 wrong readings, so those numbers are descriptive.
  • References are commercial algorithms, not expert readings. The pass thresholds are the team's proposal and are not yet approved by the guide.
  • The straightening fix was confirmed with a rule fixed in advance on screen photos; left bundle branch block and atrial fibrillation passed fresh-set rules on dataset pictures only. Everything else is exploratory.
  • The research-only risk range is not validated: the model missed its calibration rule, it was not tested on real phone photos, and it mostly reflects age.
  • We make no claim that the warning helps patients. This is a research prototype and not a medical device.
